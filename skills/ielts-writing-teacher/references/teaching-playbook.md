# IELTS Writing Teaching Playbook

## 1. Diagnosis before teaching

For any student response, classify in this order:

1. **Task failure first.** Is the prompt fully addressed? Is there an overview (T1
   Academic)? Are all three bullet points covered (T1 GT)? Is the position clear and
   developed (T2)? If not, fix this before polishing language — it is the biggest
   band lever and carries caps.
2. **Organisation second.** Is there clear progression? Does each paragraph have one
   central topic? Are ideas sequenced logically *inside* paragraphs?
3. **Language third.** Range and accuracy of vocabulary and grammar, error frequency
   and communicative effect, spelling, punctuation.
4. **Exam technique last.** Timing, word count, planning, checking.

Never teach grammar to a student whose essay answers the wrong question.

## 2. Upgrade guides

### Band 5 → 6

- **T1 Academic:** write a real overview (1 sentence: main trend/change/comparison);
  select key features instead of listing every number; support them with data.
- **T1 GT:** cover all three bullets explicitly; make the purpose clear; choose and
  sustain the right register (formal / semi-formal / informal); correct letter format.
- **T2:** answer every part of the prompt; state the position in the introduction and
  keep it consistent; develop each main idea with an explanation plus example.
- **CC:** use paragraphs with one central topic; add logical sequencers and referencing.
- **LR/GRA:** reduce repetition; mix simple and complex sentences; aim for accuracy in
  simple sentences before risking complex ones.

### Band 6 → 7 (the hardest jump)

- **T1 Academic:** overview must be clear, not just attempted; categorise data and
  identify main trends/differences; extend key features with well-chosen figures;
  avoid mechanical detail-listing.
- **T2:** ideas must be *extended and supported*, not just listed — explanation +
  example + result; avoid over-generalisation; precision in supporting material.
- **CC:** clear progression throughout the whole response; flexible use of cohesive
  devices (not mechanical "Firstly/Secondly/In conclusion" only); reference and
  substitution used accurately; paragraphing effective with logical sequencing inside
  paragraphs.
- **LR:** show awareness of style and collocation; a few accurate less-common/idiomatic
  items; only a few spelling/word-formation errors.
- **GRA:** a variety of complex structures used with some flexibility; error-free
  sentences frequent; errors do not impede communication.

### Band 7 → 8

- **TR/TA:** explore the prompt in depth; nuance the position; fully developed,
  precisely relevant support; only occasional omissions.
- **CC:** message followed with ease; cohesion very rarely attracts attention;
  skilful paragraphing; no lapses in logical progression.
- **LR:** wide, precise, natural control; skilful uncommon/idiomatic items; only
  occasional inaccuracies in word choice/collocation.
- **GRA:** majority of sentences error-free; errors **non-systematic** (fix repeated
  article/preposition/verb-agreement errors); punctuation well managed.
- **Key insight:** systematic, repeated errors are what keep a Band 7 candidate at 7.

## 3. Task-specific teaching points

### Academic Task 1
- **Overview** = the big picture: overall trend, main change, or biggest differences.
  An introduction restating the prompt is *not* an overview. Place it early or last.
- Select **key features** (major trends, steps, changes) and support with data; do not
  report every figure mechanically.
- Compare/contrast where relevant; group data sensibly.
- Format: full connected prose, ~150+ words, no bullets/headings/greeting/sign-off,
  formal or neutral style. Paragraphs matter mainly at Bands 8–9.
- Common failure: describing details accurately but never saying what the whole chart
  shows → Band 5–6 ceiling.

### General Training Task 1
- Identify the situation, recipient and required **tone** (informal friend / semi-formal
  colleague or manager / formal stranger). Sustain it consistently.
- Answer all **three bullet points** and extend each appropriately. If a bullet asks
  for "suggestions" or "problems", give more than one.
- State the purpose early. Use greeting and sign-off matched to the register.
- Common failure: right content, wrong register (too chatty to a manager) → Band 5–6.

### Task 2 (both modules)
- Decode the prompt: topic + task words + all parts + question type. Answer exactly
  what is asked; do not write generally about the topic.
- Position: clear, consistent, visible in introduction and conclusion.
- Body paragraphs: one main idea each; develop with **explanation → example → result**;
  keep examples relevant (own experience allowed).
- Balance: if a "discuss both views + your opinion" prompt, cover both views *and*
  give your opinion.
- Timed structure: ~5 min plan, ~30–32 min writing, ~3–5 min checking. Aim 260–290 words.
- Common failure: memorised introductions, over-generalised claims, ideas listed but
  not developed, one-sided answers.

## 4. Error priorities (highest band impact first)

1. Wrong or incomplete task response (including missing overview / bullet / prompt part).
2. No clear position or no development of ideas.
3. Broken progression / paragraphing (especially T2).
4. Frequent or systematic grammar errors that impede meaning.
5. Noticeable spelling/word-formation errors causing reader difficulty.
6. Restricted, repetitive or inappropriate vocabulary.
7. Mechanical overuse of linkers; faulty punctuation.

## 5. Lesson flow (one teaching point per lesson)

1. **Diagnose** — graded sample or student's own writing; identify the single biggest
   band blocker.
2. **Explain** — in descriptor language: what the band says, what the candidate's text
   does, why they differ.
3. **Model** — show a short Band 7+ example of just that feature (an overview, a
   developed paragraph, a highlighted bullet).
4. **Guided practice** — student rewrites one paragraph/overview with a checklist.
5. **Independent practice** — new prompt, timed, no help.
6. **Feedback** — re-grade just the target criterion; end with one concrete next step.

## 6. Feedback principles

- Praise something specific first, then the single highest-impact fix.
- Quote the student's words; never say "grammar is bad" without an example.
- Name the band for each criterion and justify it with descriptor wording.
- Give a rewrite target, not just a verdict ("Add a 1-sentence overview stating the
  overall trend and the biggest difference").
- Do not rewrite the whole essay for them — students improve by revising their own text.
- Remind them that a predicted band is an estimate, not an official mark.

## 7. The Mentors Lecture 1–5 framework (integrated)

The student's course handouts provide a consistent framework. Use it, but correct the handouts'
own sample errors (see below).

### Task 1 — Lectures 1–3
- **Report, not essay.** Overview (not conclusion); key features only; **report/exhibit, never
  justify**; no outside information.
- **Structure:** 3–4 paragraphs including the introduction. Standard: Intro · Overview · Body 1 ·
  Body 2. The handout's wording explains why both "3" and "4" appear in the course material.
- **Introduction:** paraphrase + units + period. Frames: `shows/illustrates the trends in...`;
  `gives/provides/reveals/presents information about...`; `compares/contrasts...`;
  `shows how the sales changed`.
- **Body:** broad groups/trends, one central topic per paragraph, topic sentence first, general
  trend supported by specific data. Detail openers: `Turning to the details...`,
  `At first glance it is clear...`
- **Data vs trend:** data = individual figures; trend = sustained movement over **more than one**
  period. Name the trend in the overview.
- **Vocabulary:** rise/fall verbs and nouns, adverbs of amount, approximation, percentage/fraction
  equivalents, and prepositions (`from...to`, `at`, `by`, `of`).
- **Processes:** follow the arrows; present simple passive; omit the agent; sequencing adverbs;
  cycles end with `the cycle repeats itself`.
- **Maps:** past simple passive with `in`, past perfect passive with `by`, spatial-order vocabulary.

### Task 2 — Lectures 4–5
- **Five types:** argument · cause/effect · discussion · advantages/disadvantages · double
  question. The same topic generates different requirements — decode the exact question.
- **Timing:** 40 min = 10 plan / 25 write / 5 revise; audience = educated non-specialist.
- **Paragraphs:** 4–5 minimum; one central topic per body paragraph; topic sentence first.
- **Thesis:** one sentence at the end of the introduction; clear side + reasons. No rhetorical
  questions, no memorised outline sentences (`This essay would like to explore...`).
- **Paraphrasing:** synonyms (mind their strength), word-form changes, clause reordering.
- **Discussion type:** two view paragraphs, opinion in conclusion; balanced or one-sided.
- **Advantages/disadvantages:** no opinion for `Discuss...`; explicit verdict for `outweigh`.
- **Causes/effects:** add solutions **only** if asked; causes-and-solutions prompts omit effects.
- **Double question:** give **equal weight** to both questions — missing one caps TR at 5.
- **Counter-argument:** acknowledge the opposing view, then refute it with `although`/`however`.
- **Examples:** clear, concise, realistic — helpful but **not mandatory**; explanation also works.
- **Conclusion:** summarise only, never a new point.
- **Editing checklist:** tenses/agreement · articles/prepositions/word formation · sentence range ·
  vocabulary · spelling · topic sentences · connectives · paragraph development · evidence ·
  point of view · task relevance.

### Warning about the handouts
Their sample texts contain figure mismatches, garbled sentences and a genuine Band 5 essay with
examiner comments. Treat the **framework** as authoritative; verify every figure, article and
linker before presenting sample wording to a student.
