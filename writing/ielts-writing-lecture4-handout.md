## 1. Overview of Task 2

- Candidates write a composition on a specific topic, usually **social, environmental or economic**
  issues. Topics are general — **no specialist knowledge** is needed.
- Style: **academic**, aimed at an **educated non-specialist** audience. Avoid jargon.
- Five question types (the handout demonstrates all five using one topic):

| Type | Question ending | What it requires |
|---|---|---|
| **Argument** | *To what extent do you agree or disagree?* | Opinion/strong position; educate and persuade; show your argument has merit |
| **Cause and Effect** | *Why is this the case? What effect does this have...?* | Reasons + effects |
| **Comparison and Contrast / Discussion** | *Discuss both views and give your opinion* | Explain both views, then give your opinion; balanced treatment |
| **Advantages and Disadvantages** | *What are the advantages and disadvantages?* | Benefits and drawbacks; **no opinion required** |
| **Double question** | *Is this a positive or negative development?* | Factors + your judgement of positive/negative |

**Base topic used in the handout:** *Nowadays, more people make online payments through various
apps than ever before. Some say that using mobile phone apps is more convenient than other payment
methods.* — note how the same statement generates five different tasks.

> ⚠️ **Band 8–9 correction:** the handout says an advantages/disadvantages essay should not include
> an opinion. That is true **only** for *"Discuss the advantages and disadvantages"*. If the
> question asks *"Do the advantages outweigh the disadvantages?"*, you **must** give a clear
> overall judgement — see your Task 2 class 2 notes.

## 2. Band descriptors — how to increase each score

Four criteria, each scored 1–9; the average is the Task 2 band.

**Task Response** — your reply to the question, principal ideas and their development. Increase it by:
- paying attention to **all** issues in the question
- writing about the **issues**, not just the general topic
- answering the question with relevant main points
- planning supporting points so they do not go off topic
- writing at least 250 words

**Coherence and Cohesion** — organisation, paragraphs, linking words. Increase it by:
- having **four or five paragraphs, but not less** (two or three body paragraphs are possible)
- one central topic in each body paragraph
- organising ideas logically
- using a range of linking words
- avoiding errors in linking

**Lexical Resource** — vocabulary, paraphrasing, spelling, number of mistakes. Increase it by:
- paying attention to **collocations** (which verb matches which noun)
- paraphrasing carefully to avoid errors
- using a range of words appropriate to the topic
- spelling correctly
- avoiding errors and avoiding informal language and contractions

**Grammar Range and Accuracy** — sentence structures, tenses, grammar control, mistakes. Increase it by:
- using different grammar structures
- paying attention to tenses
- varying word order
- using correct punctuation
- avoiding common errors: **articles, plural nouns, word order**

## 3. Time plan and audience

Typical instruction: *You should spend about 40 minutes on this task. Present a written argument or
case to an educated non-specialist audience... You should write at least 250 words. Use your own
ideas, knowledge and experience, and support your argument with examples and relevant evidence.*

Handout time split: **brainstorming 10 min → writing 25 min → revising 5 min**.

Target: educated **non-specialist** — be understandable, not technical.

## 4. Essay type 1 — Advantages and Disadvantages

**Structure (handout):**
- **Introduction:** background statement · paraphrase the question · overview sentence giving an
  idea of the body paragraphs. **No thesis/opinion needed** for the pure "discuss" version.
- **Body:** two body paragraphs — one for advantages, one for disadvantages. Start with a topic
  sentence highlighting the main focus; mention one or two points per paragraph and support them
  with relevant examples/facts/descriptions.
- **Conclusion:** rewrite the overview sentence; summarise main ideas; **no new points**.

**Handout sample (working from home), condensed:**

> Instead of travelling to work, more people nowadays work from home using computers connected to
> the internet. Although this trend has major benefits, it also has drawbacks.
>
> There are clear advantages to working from home. Firstly, people no longer spend a large amount
> of time commuting — often an hour or more each way — which is a major cause of stress. By
> avoiding the daily journey, they can save money and work more effectively. Moreover, working
> from home gives people more control over their lives: instead of wasting time in an office, they
> can decide when and how to complete the work they have been given.
>
> On the other hand, working from home can be lonely. People usually befriend their colleagues and
> rely on them to meet their social needs. Without co-workers to chat with or help solve a problem
> that cannot be fixed online, workers can feel isolated. Even email or video calls cannot fully
> replace face-to-face problem-solving, and an office helps create a team atmosphere that is
> better for productivity.
>
> To conclude, working from home can make a worker's life easier and give them more control, but
> some tasks are better done with other people around, and staying at home too long may create
> loneliness.

## 5. Paraphrasing — three techniques

The introduction is basically a paraphrase of the prompt. **Never copy** — copied words do not count
towards the word count. Understand the gist first, then re-express it.

1. **Use synonyms** — but watch strength: `interesting` ≠ `fascinating` (stronger).
2. **Change word forms** — noun → verb, verb → noun, adjective → noun.
   - Original: *Some people think Facebook is an **invasion** of privacy.*
   - Paraphrase: *Some people think Facebook has **invaded** our privacy.*
3. **Change sentence structure** — passive ↔ active, or reorder clauses.
   - Original: *It is difficult to say whether the economy will improve.*
   - Paraphrase: *Whether the economy will improve is difficult to say.*

## 6. Key essay-writing principles from the handout

- **Overview variations:** for advantages/disadvantages essays there is **no thesis statement** —
  instead write a statement showing the topics the body paragraphs will cover.
- **Topic sentence supremacy:** the first sentence of every body paragraph should be the topic
  sentence. Each paragraph has **one clear central topic**; the topic sentence tells the reader
  what that idea is. This earns Coherence marks.
- **Supporting details:** Band 7 Task Response requires ideas to be *presented, extended and
  supported*. Examples help illustrate and extend ideas — but each example must be **clear, easy
  to follow, concise and realistic**. Examples are **not mandatory**; explanation is another way.
- **Conclusion:** summarise the whole essay briefly in one sentence. **Never introduce a new point**
  in the last paragraph — a point that did not appear in the body is inappropriate and irrelevant.

## 7. Essay type 2 — Argumentative

- Examiners want a **clear opinion**: decide which side you support before writing.
- Provide enough points to develop your side, but **also mention the other side**.
- Handout example: *In some countries, many people suffer health problems from eating too much
  fast food. It is therefore necessary for governments to impose a higher tax on this kind of food.
  Do you agree or disagree?*

**Structure:**
- **Introduction:** background statement · paraphrase · **thesis statement** stating directly
  which side you take and why.
- **Body:** several body paragraphs — each point supporting your side gets its own paragraph,
  beginning with a topic sentence and supported by examples/facts/descriptions. Add **one paragraph
  of counter-argument** acknowledging the other side and explaining why you did not take it.
- **Conclusion:** restate the thesis, summarise main ideas, **no new points**.

**On "partly agree":** the handout warns that so-called "experts" advise partial agreement but
says it is **not a requirement and not recommended**, because it often produces no clear focus and
a confusing structure.

> ⚠️ **Band 8–9 balance:** IELTS officially permits a qualified position (*largely agree, with
> reservations*). What it does not permit is a **vague or contradictory** position. Decide your
> main side first, state it clearly, then nuance it — never let the nuance obscure the verdict.

## 8. Thesis statement

- Probably the **most important part of the introduction**: one sentence that tells the reader what
  the essay is about, which points form the body, and your opinion/position.
- **Placed at the end** of the introductory paragraph, after the paraphrase.
- Example topic: banning rickshaws in Dhaka City. Supporting points: slow, a cause of congestion,
  cannot satisfy long-distance travel.
- Handout thesis: *"As rickshaws are slow, are the primary reasons of traffic congestion and not
  capable of satisfying long distance travelling, I believe that rickshaws should be banned from
  Dhaka city."*

**Band 8–9 correction:** the handout sentence has an agreement/parallelism error. Write:
> *As rickshaws are slow, are a major cause of traffic congestion, and cannot meet long-distance
> travel needs, I believe they should be banned from Dhaka City.*

**Rules for a thesis statement:**
- No rhetorical questions — the thesis is not a question.
- Say clearly what the essay is about; be brief and decisive.
- Give reasons for your opinion where possible.
- Place it after paraphrasing the question.
- **Never write an outline sentence** — these look clichéd and memorised, e.g. *"This essay would
  like to explore reasons for this in more detail."* Examiners are trained to spot memorised phrases.

## 9. Counter-argument

- A counter-argument acknowledges standpoints against your argument, then re-affirms your own as
  the most logical solution — typically using `although` or `however`.
- It shows you can understand and respect views that differ from yours, and that your position was
  reached after evaluating both sides. It strengthens, rather than weakens, your essay.

## 10. Band 8–9 checklist from Lecture 4

- [ ] Identify the exact question type — requirements differ even with the same topic
- [ ] Advantages/disadvantages: no opinion for "discuss"; explicit verdict for "outweigh"
- [ ] Argumentative: clear side + counter-argument paragraph
- [ ] Four or five paragraphs; one central topic per body paragraph
- [ ] Introduction paraphrased with three techniques; no copied wording
- [ ] Thesis at the end of the introduction for argumentative essays; no outline sentences
- [ ] Topic sentence first in every body paragraph; ideas extended and supported
- [ ] Examples clear, concise, realistic — or explain the point instead
- [ ] Conclusion summarises only; no new ideas
- [ ] Collocations, articles, plurals and word order checked; no contractions
- [ ] 270–290 words in ~40 minutes (10 plan / 25 write / 5 revise)
