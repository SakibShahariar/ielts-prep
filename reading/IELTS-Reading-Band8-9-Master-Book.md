# IELTS Reading — Band 8–9 Master Book

**Every question type. How to face it. Tricks, tips and traps.**

> Target: Band 8–9. Based on the official IELTS Reading formats and scoring, the official
> question-type lists, and established IELTS teaching practice. Verified November 2026.
> Works for both Academic and General Training — the techniques are the same; only the
> texts differ.

---

## How to use this book

1. **Part 1** tells you the rules of the test. Read once, memorise the answer-form rules.
2. **Part 2** gives you the four core skills. Do not skip this — every question type depends on them.
3. **Part 3** is the main event: one chapter per question type. Read the chapter *before* you practise that type.
4. **Part 4** is a deep dive into the hardest question types.
5. **Part 5** is your timing and exam-day plan.
6. **Part 6** teaches you how to analyse your mistakes and includes a full worked passage set.
7. Keep a **paraphrase log** as you go (see Chapter 7). This is the single habit that raises bands.

# PART 1 — KNOW THE TEST

## Chapter 1 — Format

| | **Academic** | **General Training** |
|---|---|---|
| Time | 60 minutes | 60 minutes |
| Texts | 3 passages, 2,150–2,750 words total; books, journals, magazines, newspapers, online; general-interest undergraduate/postgraduate topics; at least one contains detailed logical argument | 3 sections, 2,150–2,375 words total; increasing difficulty — Section 1 everyday/social texts (notices, ads, timetables), Section 2 workplace texts (job descriptions, contracts, training), Section 3 one longer general-interest text |
| Questions | 40 | 40 |
| Marking | 1 mark per correct answer; no negative marking | same |

Key facts:
- Technical vocabulary in Academic texts is glossed if needed.
- The question types and techniques are essentially the same in both tests.
- From mid-2026 IELTS is computer-delivered only. In Reading you see a clock and can
  highlight text. There is **no extra transfer time** in Reading (unlike paper Listening).
- You can tackle passages and questions in any order.

## Chapter 2 — Scoring and bands

Each correct answer = 1 mark, out of 40. Wrong answers cost nothing.

IELTS publishes **average** marks needed at each band; exact cut-offs vary slightly by test version:

| Band | Academic (avg. correct) | GT (avg. correct) |
|---|---|---|
| 5 | 15 | 4 |
| 6 | 23 | 5 |
| 7 | 30 | 6 |
| 8 | 35 | 7 |
| 9 | ~39–40 (approx.) | ~39–40 (approx.) |

- GT usually needs **more** correct answers for the same band because its texts are easier.
- Band 9 is typically around 39–40 correct, but IELTS does not publish a fixed average here.
- For **Band 8** aim for ~35+ correct; for **Band 9** aim for ~39–40 correct. Leave no blanks.

## Chapter 3 — Answer-form rules (memorise these)

- **No negative marking.** A wrong answer earns no point — always guess. A blank is a guaranteed zero.
- Answers are **100% correct or wrong**. No partial credit.
- **Spelling and grammar count.** A wrong plural or misspelling loses the mark. Words in brackets in an answer key are optional — you may include or omit them.
- Respect the **word limit** ("NO MORE THAN TWO WORDS"). Exceeding it is wrong.
- **Capitalisation does not matter:** `TRUE`, `True`, `true` are all accepted.
- Abbreviations accepted: `T / F / NG` and `Y / N / NG`.
- **Never mix systems:** `True` instead of `Yes`, or `Y` instead of `T`, is wrong.
- Commas are unnecessary in multi-word answers. Brackets such as `pen(s)` are not allowed from candidates.
- You may change answers any time in the 60 minutes, but there is no extra check time afterwards.
- Use the computer test's highlight tool to mark keywords.

## Chapter 4 — The question types at a glance

Fourteen practical types (the official Academic list has 11; GT has 8; completion formats and
T/F/NG vs Y/N/NG are counted separately here):

1. Multiple Choice
2. True / False / Not Given
3. Yes / No / Not Given
4. Matching Information (matching paragraphs)
5. Matching Headings
6. Matching Features / Classification
7. Matching Sentence Endings
8. Sentence Completion
9. Summary Completion
10. Note / Table / Flow-chart Completion
11. Diagram Label Completion
12. Short Answer Questions
13. List Selection
14. Choosing a Title

### The order-of-answers rule

| Answers FOLLOW passage order | Answers DO NOT follow passage order |
|---|---|
| T/F/NG · Y/N/NG · multiple choice · list selection · sentence completion · summary completion · short answer · matching sentence endings (beginnings) | Matching headings · matching information (paragraphs) · matching features/classification · flow-chart completion · diagram labels |

Use this to narrow your search area. If answers follow order, once you find Q2 you know Q3
is below it — never search backwards.

# PART 2 — THE CORE SKILLS

## Chapter 5 — Skimming

Skimming = reading for the general idea and layout, **not** for detail.

- Spend **2–3 minutes** skimming each passage before answering its questions.
- Read the title, the first sentence of each paragraph, and the last paragraph.
- Note the topic of each paragraph in the margin (on computer, note it mentally).
- Underline keywords as you go.
- Exception: **matching headings** questions appear *before* the passage — read the
  headings first, then skim with those headings in mind.

Do not try to understand the passage. Your job is to know where things are.

## Chapter 6 — Scanning

Scanning = locating specific information quickly. It is mechanical, not comprehension.

- Move your eyes across the text looking for the keyword or its paraphrase.
- Names, dates, numbers and places are easy anchors.
- Ignore everything that does not match.
- If you cannot find the keyword after a quick sweep, look for a synonym or a
  differently-structured phrase.
- Practice moving your eyes faster over long texts — this is a trainable physical skill.

## Chapter 7 — Keywords and paraphrase (the heart of Reading)

IELTS rarely copies the question wording into the passage. Almost every answer is paraphrased.

**Method:**
1. Underline **every** keyword in the question. Missing one (e.g. "abroad", "currently",
   "most") changes the answer.
2. Prepare paraphrases before scanning: `work → employment`, `children → young learners`,
   `changing → altering`, `effect → impact`.
3. Paraphrase may be a completely different phrasing, not a single synonym.
4. Scan for the keyword **or** its paraphrase.
5. Match the **whole meaning** of the sentence and its context — not individual words.

**Warning:** the same words can appear in the passage with a different meaning. Word-matching
is the number-one cause of wrong answers.

Keep a **paraphrase log**: question wording → passage wording, recorded after every practice set.

## Chapter 8 — Analysis

Once you have located the relevant sentence, read it (and sometimes the whole paragraph)
carefully. This matters most for:
- True/False/Not Given and Yes/No/Not Given
- Multiple choice
- Matching headings

Check grammar clues: time expressions, quantifiers (`most`, `all`, `some`), comparatives,
modality (`plans to` vs `currently does`). These flip meanings.

## Chapter 9 — The 7-step method for any question

1. Read the instructions — answer format, word limit, reuse rules.
2. Identify the question type and recall its rule (order? one heading per paragraph?).
3. Underline keywords and prepare paraphrases.
4. Decide where to look (use the order rule).
5. Scan for keyword/paraphrase.
6. Analyse the located sentence against the question.
7. Check form: word limit, spelling, plural, answer label (T vs Y).

# PART 3 — THE QUESTION-TYPE PLAYBOOK

## Chapter 10 — Multiple Choice

**What it asks:** choose the correct option (usually A/B/C/D) for a question or sentence ending.

**How to face it:**
1. Underline keywords in the **question stem and every option**.
2. Paraphrase the options — distractors often repeat passage words but change the meaning.
3. Use the order rule to find the relevant part of the passage.
4. Match meaning, not words. Eliminate options that contradict the passage.
5. If two options seem close, check the exact quantifier/time/comparative.

**Tricks & tips:**
- The correct answer is usually paraphrased; wrong options often use exact wording from the passage in a different context.
- Answers follow passage order.
- Never choose an option just because it "sounds right" — find evidence.

**Traps:** ignoring the word "NOT" or "EXCEPT" in the stem; choosing a familiar word.

**Mini example**
> Passage: "The scheme was originally limited to volunteers, but after 2005 it was opened to all residents."
> Question: When could all residents join the scheme?
> A. Before 2005 · B. From 2005 · C. Never
> **Answer: B.** "Opened to all residents" = from 2005 onwards. Option A is the old rule.

## Chapter 11 — True / False / Not Given

**What it asks:** decide whether a statement agrees with the passage, contradicts it, or is absent.

- **True:** the same information is found (often paraphrased).
- **False:** the passage gives different/contradicting information.
- **Not Given:** the whole meaning is not in the passage.

**How to face it:**
1. Analyse the statement before searching. Break it into parts.
2. Underline every keyword — especially time, quantity and comparison words.
3. Paraphrase the statement.
4. Scan using the order rule.
5. Check every part of the statement against the passage.
6. Apply the strict definitions above.

**Tricks & tips:**
- Answers follow passage order.
- Some keywords may appear in the passage while the whole claim is absent → Not Given.
- False means the passage tells you the statement is wrong, not merely that you cannot find it.
- Use the whole passage context, not one sentence in isolation.
- No outside knowledge — answer only according to the passage.
- `T / F / NG` is accepted on the answer sheet.

**Traps:** False vs Not Given; ignoring one keyword; treating a comparison that isn't there.

**Worked trap**
> Passage: "Most working-class residents used the tunnel for functional purposes; middle-class residents used it mainly as a tourist attraction."
> Statement: "More working-class people used the tunnel than middle-class people."
> **Answer: Not Given.** The passage says *why* each group used it, not *how many*. The statement introduces a comparison that is absent.

## Chapter 12 — Yes / No / Not Given

**What it asks:** the same decisions, but about the **writer's views or claims** rather than factual information.

**How to face it:** identical technique to T/F/NG, but ask: "Does the writer say this is their view?"
- Some passages clearly separate the writer's opinion from reported opinions of others — watch attribution ("critics argue...", "the author concludes...").

**Tricks & tips:** use only `Y / N / NG`; never mix with True/False. More common in Academic than GT.

## Chapter 13 — Matching Information (paragraph matching)

**What it asks:** match a statement to the paragraph that contains that information.

**How to face it:**
1. Paraphrase the statement.
2. Scan the passage — answers **do not come in order**.
3. Match the specific detail, not the paragraph's main idea.
4. Check instructions: may a letter be used more than once? Are all paragraphs used?

**Tricks & tips:** start with the easiest, most distinctive statement (a name, date or number) to build momentum.

**Trap:** confusing this with matching headings — headings test the *main idea*, this tests a *detail*.

## Chapter 14 — Matching Headings

**What it asks:** choose a heading for each paragraph/section from a list.

**How to face it:**
1. Read the heading list **before** the passage (the list is printed above it).
2. Analyse each heading: circle the key idea and note what makes it different from the others.
3. Skim each paragraph for its **main idea**, not a supporting detail.
4. Match one heading per paragraph. There are usually **extra headings**.
5. Answers **do not come in order**.

**Tricks & tips:**
- A heading that covers only one sentence is wrong.
- The first and last sentences of a paragraph usually reveal its purpose.
- Eliminate headings you have already used.
- Answers are Roman numerals — check the instructions.

**Trap:** choosing a heading because one word matches; the paragraph's *aim* is different.

## Chapter 15 — Matching Features / Classification

**What it asks:** decide which category (person, place, theory, period) a statement belongs to.

**How to face it:**
1. Read the category list and the statements.
2. Paraphrase each statement.
3. Scan — answers **do not come in order**.
4. Match carefully to the exact claim or feature.

**Tricks & tips:** look for reporting verbs and attribution ("X argued...", "Y disputed...").

**Trap:** assigning a statement to the name mentioned *near* it rather than the one it belongs to.

## Chapter 16 — Matching Sentence Endings

**What it asks:** match each sentence beginning with the correct ending from a list.

**How to face it:**
1. Read all beginnings, then all endings.
2. Underline keywords and paraphrase.
3. Find the information in the passage — beginnings **follow passage order**.
4. Choose the ending that matches the meaning **and** makes a grammatical sentence.
5. There are usually more endings than you need.

**Trick:** eliminate endings that produce an ungrammatical sentence — grammar is a free filter.

## Chapter 17 — Completion questions (four formats)

This chapter covers **sentence completion, summary completion, note/table/flow-chart completion and diagram label completion**.

**How to face them all:**
1. Read the instructions: words from the passage or from a box? Word limit?
2. Identify the **part of speech** needed for each gap — noun, verb, adjective, plural?
3. Scan for the information — most completion answers follow passage order (flow charts and diagrams sometimes do not).
4. Copy the exact word from the passage if instructed; check spelling and plural.
5. Read the completed sentence/table to confirm it is grammatical and logical.

**Format-specific tips:**
- **Sentence completion:** grammar of the sentence tells you the word form.
- **Summary completion:** the summary follows the passage's logic; use order to locate.
- **Table completion:** read the column headings first — they define what each cell needs.
- **Flow-chart completion:** follow the arrows; answers may not be in order.
- **Diagram labels:** information is usually concentrated in one or two paragraphs; check the word limit.

**Traps:** changing word form; exceeding the word limit; using a synonym when the passage word is required.

## Chapter 18 — Short Answer Questions

**What it asks:** answer detail questions with words from the passage.

**How to face it:**
1. Identify the type of answer required (name, number, place, reason).
2. Paraphrase the question.
3. Scan using the order rule.
4. Copy the exact words, respecting the word limit.
5. Check spelling and plural.

**Trick:** the answer is usually a short noun phrase, not a full sentence.

## Chapter 19 — List Selection

**What it asks:** choose items from one long list (usually A–G) that match given descriptions.

**How to face it:**
1. Read the whole list first and paraphrase each option.
2. Underline keywords in each question.
3. Locate information in the passage — answers follow order.
4. Check whether each option may be used more than once.

**Trap:** assuming each letter is used exactly once.

## Chapter 20 — Choosing a Title

**What it asks:** pick the best title for the whole passage.

**How to face it:**
1. Compare the differences between the titles — they are usually close.
2. Focus on the opening and closing paragraphs.
3. Reject titles that cover only one paragraph.
4. Do not spend long — this is one mark.

**Trick:** ask "Which title covers the whole argument, not just one part?"

# PART 4 — THE HARD ONES

## Chapter 21 — False vs Not Given: a decision tree

1. Can I find the **whole meaning** of the statement in the passage (paraphrased)? → **TRUE**
2. Does the passage state something that **contradicts** the statement? → **FALSE**
3. Is the whole meaning **absent**, even though some words appear? → **NOT GIVEN**

Grammar traps that create False/Not Given errors:

| Passage wording | Statement | Answer | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| "By the second half of the 17th century..." | "After the 17th century" | **False** | Second half is still inside the century |
| "Plans to transfer gas by pipeline" | "Gas is now being shipped abroad" | **False** | Plan ≠ current reality |
| "Most working-class people used it for functional purposes" | "More working-class people used it" | **Not Given** | No comparison is made |
| "Faulty shoes can be refunded" | "Unwanted shoes can be refunded" | **Not Given** | Only faulty shoes are covered |

Rule: identify the keyword you **ignored** — it is almost always the reason for the error.

## Chapter 22 — Matching headings: main idea vs detail

- The right heading summarises the **purpose** of the paragraph.
- A single detail, however prominently placed, is not the main idea.
- The opening and closing sentences usually state the aim.
- If two headings both fit, choose the one that covers **more** of the paragraph.
- Use elimination: remove headings that clearly belong elsewhere.

## Chapter 23 — Multiple choice: dissecting distractors

Typical distractor types:
1. **Word-match trap:** uses exact words from the passage but in a different context.
2. **Over-generalisation:** "all", "always", "never" when the passage says "some", "often".
3. **Wrong time/period:** mixes an earlier situation with a later one.
4. **Reversed causation:** the passage says A caused B; the option says B caused A.
5. **Outside knowledge:** plausible but not stated.

For every option, ask: "Which sentence proves or disproves this?" If you cannot find one, reject it.

# PART 5 — TIMING AND EXAM DAY

## Chapter 24 — The 60-minute plan

| Stage | Time |
|---|---|
| Passage 1: skim + questions | 17–18 min |
| Passage 2: skim + questions | 20 min |
| Passage 3: skim + questions | 20–22 min |
| Check answers / word limits | 3–5 min |

Rules:
- Skim each passage in **2–3 minutes** before its questions.
- If stuck for more than ~90 seconds, mark and move on. Return later.
- Do your strongest passage first if that helps your confidence.
- All answers must be written before the hour ends — there is no transfer time.
- Guess every unanswered question in the final minute. No penalty for wrong answers.

## Chapter 25 — Exam-day checklist

- [ ] Read instructions for every question set — especially reuse rules and word limits.
- [ ] Confirm T/F/NG vs Y/N/NG before writing labels.
- [ ] Underline every keyword before scanning.
- [ ] Use the highlight tool (computer test) for keywords.
- [ ] Watch the clock at each passage boundary.
- [ ] Check plural, spelling and word limits in completion answers.
- [ ] Never leave a blank.

# PART 6 — PRACTICE AND ERROR ANALYSIS

## Chapter 26 — The error taxonomy

Classify every wrong answer:

| Cause | Fix |
|---|---|
| Vocabulary/paraphrase gap | Build paraphrase log; broad reading |
| Word-matching | Match whole meaning; rephrase the sentence |
| Missed keyword | Underline every keyword before scanning |
| False vs Not Given confusion | Apply definitions strictly; check every part |
| Order assumption | Learn which types follow order |
| Time pressure | Timing drills; skip-and-return rule |
| Instruction error | Read instructions twice |
| Grammar/form error | Check the sentence after inserting the answer |

## Chapter 27 — Analyse, don't accumulate

Do not simply do test after test. Instead:
1. Do a small set of questions.
2. Analyse **every** answer, right and wrong.
3. Record passage evidence and the paraphrase used.
4. Log recurring error causes.
5. Drill the weakest question type in isolation.
6. Then do a full timed test to measure progress.

Use authentic IELTS Cambridge test books for real past papers.

## Chapter 28 — Full worked practice set

**Passage (original practice text)**

**A** In the mid-nineteenth century, rapid industrialisation transformed many European cities. Streets were crowded, air was polluted, and working-class families had almost no access to open space. Reformers argued that green areas were not a luxury but a necessity, and their campaigns led to the creation of the first public parks.

**B** The earliest municipal park, opened in Liverpool in 1842, was designed by Joseph Paxton. Its layout deliberately combined formal gardens with wide lawns where visitors could walk, rest or play sport. Paxton believed that exposure to nature improved both physical and mental health, a view widely shared by doctors of the period.

**C** By the end of the century, similar parks had appeared across Europe and North America. However, their purpose gradually shifted. While early parks were intended mainly for recreation and moral improvement, later designs increasingly emphasised scientific study: botanical collections, museums and labelled trees became common features.

**D** Today, urban parks face new pressures. City authorities must balance the demands of sports clubs, dog owners and conservationists, while budgets for maintenance have fallen. Research suggests that regular visits to green spaces can reduce stress and improve concentration, yet many residents still live more than a kilometre from a park.

### Questions 1–4 — True / False / Not Given

1. Industrialisation created new problems for city dwellers.
2. Paxton believed that nature could improve people's health.
3. Early parks were designed primarily for scientific research.
4. Most residents visited their local park every day.

### Questions 5–7 — Multiple choice

5. What motivated the reformers mentioned in paragraph A?
   A. They wanted to improve scientific education.
   B. They believed green space was essential.
   C. They wanted to reduce traffic congestion.
6. Which feature was typical of *later* parks?
   A. Wide lawns for sport · B. Formal gardens · C. Botanical collections
7. What is one modern pressure on urban parks?
   A. Falling maintenance budgets · B. Lack of research · C. Too few visitors

### Questions 8–10 — Matching information
Match the statement with the correct paragraph, A–D.
8. A decline in available funding.
9. The first park of its kind in a particular city.
10. Evidence about the effects of green space on health.

### Questions 11–14 — Matching headings
Choose the correct heading for each paragraph from the list below.
i. A new emphasis on education and research
ii. The origins of the public park movement
iii. Competing demands and modern challenges
iv. A designer's vision and the first municipal park
v. The decline of industrial cities

11. Paragraph A · 12. Paragraph B · 13. Paragraph C · 14. Paragraph D

### Questions 15–17 — Sentence completion (ONE WORD ONLY)
15. The first municipal park opened in ______ in 1842.
16. Later parks included labelled trees and ______.
17. Many residents live more than one ______ from a park.

### Questions 18–20 — Classification
Write R (reformers), P (Paxton) or C (city authorities).
18. Argued that green space was a necessity.
19. Designed an early municipal park.
20. Must balance competing demands today.

### Answer key with evidence

1. **TRUE** — A: "Streets were crowded, air was polluted, and working-class families had almost no access to open space."
2. **TRUE** — B: "Paxton believed that exposure to nature improved both physical and mental health."
3. **FALSE** — C: "early parks were intended mainly for recreation and moral improvement" — scientific study came later.
4. **NOT GIVEN** — the passage gives no information about how often residents visit parks.
5. **B** — A: "Reformers argued that green areas were not a luxury but a necessity."
6. **C** — C: "later designs increasingly emphasised scientific study: botanical collections, museums and labelled trees."
7. **A** — D: "budgets for maintenance have fallen."
8. **D** — "budgets for maintenance have fallen."
9. **B** — "The earliest municipal park, opened in Liverpool in 1842..."
10. **D** — "Research suggests that regular visits to green spaces can reduce stress and improve concentration."
11. **ii** — A describes the origins of the movement.
12. **iv** — B is about Paxton and the first municipal park.
13. **i** — C describes the shift to scientific study.
14. **iii** — D is about modern competing demands. (Heading v is the distractor: industrialisation is mentioned but the paragraph is not about its decline.)
15. **Liverpool** — B.
16. **museums** (or botanical collections / labelled trees) — C.
17. **kilometre** — D: "more than a kilometre from a park."
18. **R** — A.
19. **P** — B.
20. **C** — D.

## Chapter 29 — From Band 8 to Band 9

Band 8 and Band 9 are both "fully operational command", but the difference in Reading is
brutal: Band 8 tolerates around five wrong answers; Band 9 tolerates roughly one or none.

| | **Band 8** | **Band 9** |
|---|---|---|
| Raw score (Academic, approx.) | ~35/40 | ~39–40/40 |
| Errors | A few wrong answers allowed | Almost none |
| Speed | Comfortable within 60 minutes | Fast, near-automatic scanning |
| Paraphrase | Recognises common rephrasing | Instantly sees complex rephrasing, negation and hedging |
| Answer form | Occasional careless form errors | No avoidable errors at all |

**What actually separates 8 from 9:**

1. **Eliminate every avoidable error.** Word limits, plurals, spelling, T vs Y labels,
   reused letters, order assumptions — none of these are "language" errors, and all of
   them are free marks lost.
2. **Make scanning automatic.** At Band 9 you should locate a keyword or its paraphrase
   almost instantly. Practise speed drills, not just accuracy drills.
3. **Master complex paraphrase.** Beyond single synonyms: grammatical transformations,
   negation, hedging (`may`, `tends to`), quantifiers and time shifts.
4. **Own the whole passage.** For headings and paragraph matching, know the purpose and
   location of every paragraph — no blind searching.
5. **Never leave a blank, but never guess blindly.** Every answer should be evidenced.
6. **English level matters.** Tricks cannot take a Band 7 level of English to Band 9.
   Broad reading of difficult material (newspapers, journals, The Economist, New Scientist)
   is part of the work, not an optional extra.

**Benchmark before you expect Band 9:** consistently score 38+/40 on timed authentic
IELTS Cambridge tests. If you are at 35–36, you are solidly Band 8 — close the specific
gap identified in your error log rather than doing more full tests.


# APPENDIX — QUICK REFERENCE CARD

**Before every question set:** instructions → word limit → reuse rules → answer labels.

**Order rule:** order for T/F/NG, Y/N/NG, multiple choice, sentence/summary/short answer;
no order for headings, paragraph matching, features, flow charts, diagrams.

**T/F/NG:** whole meaning present → True; passage contradicts → False; whole meaning absent → Not Given.

**Answer form:** no negative marking · guess everything · spelling/plurals count · capitals fine · never mix T/F with Y/N · respect word limits.

**Timing:** 60 min · skim 2–3 min per passage · skip after 90 seconds · final 5 min check · no blanks.

**Band target 8+:** ~35/40 correct in Academic Reading.
